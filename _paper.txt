                                                  Abstract
          Operational land surface forecasting systems built on Mamba-family Structured
          State Space Models absorb non-stationary confounding events (unrecorded irriga-
          tion booms, dam-operation shifts, sensor recalibrations) into their state-transition
          matrices, silently biasing NDVI, LST, and crop phenology predictions long after the
          physical cause ends. This paper introduces SSU-LSF (State-Space Unlearning for
          Land Surface Forecasting), the first machine-unlearning framework purpose-built
          for geoscientific Mamba-based SSMs. We develop EKFac influence functions
          specialized to the Mamba state matrices via a closed-form matrix-exponential
          gradient, use spectral-radius-weighted elbow thresholding to localize a tempo-
          ral confounding footprint Φ, and apply Hessian-free projected gradient ascent
          within a KL-divergence trust region augmented by spatial total-variation (TV)
          regularization. Proposition 1 establishes

1    Introduction
Long-horizon forecasting of Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) [4], Land Surface
Temperature (LST), and crop phenology signals is foundational for climate adaptation and food secu-
rity [1, 2]. Fusing ERA5 reanalysis [3] with satellite phenology archives inside deep sequence models
has materially advanced forecast fidelity over classical baselines [7]. Mamba [5] and S4 [6] SSMs
have become the architecture of choice, delivering linear-time recurrent inference over multi-year
series [8, 9, 10]. Operational archives, however, are not stationary: a two-year unrecorded irrigation
project, a dam-operation policy shift, or a sensor recalibration contaminates the training window,
and the SSM state matrices (Ā, B̄, C) integrate the anomaly with genuine climate signals. After the
physical cause ends, the learned parameters continue to forward-propagate the bias (Figure 1).
Full retraining costs 47+ GPU-hours. Gradient reversal [11] degrades clean-domain RMSE by 7–22%.
EWC [12] protects weights from change, the opposite of what unlearning requires. SISA [13] requires
pre-partitioned shards before training. Two recent methods, SaLUn as ForgetFS [21] and SEMU
as MULL [22], advances approximate unlearning via saliency masking and SVD projection but
do not exploit the SSM recurrence structure and, as we show, sacrifice spatial coherence without
TV regularization. Extended related work and prior art for spatial TV in parameter space (fused
lasso [35], ROF [36]) is in Appendix A.
Following that line of work, this paper introduces SSU-LSF. Among our contributions: (i) We
establish Proposition 1 with an explicit Lipschitz bound on the full encoder (not just the forecast head;

3   Experiments
Setup. Backbone: Mamba encoder-decoder, 6 blocks (d=256, E=2, N =16), 4×4 stride-4 patch
embedding, 3-layer Gaussian MLP head (47.3M; Appendix B). We also run one experiment on an S4
backbone (Appendix P) to validate generalization beyond Mamba. Training: AdamW (lr=3×10−4 ,
cosine, 100 epochs), 4× A100 80 GB, PyTorch 2.3. Unlearning: η=5×10−5 , β=0.6, δKL =0.05,
γ=95th pct., λTV =0.01. Cost per unlearning request (EKFac re-estimated per request): 1.8 GPU-h
on NDVI-LST, 5.6 GPU-h on ERA5, 3.0 GPU-h on CropHarvest (Table 9). Eleven baselines: CM;
OR† ; SISA [13]; GR [11]; SR [11]; ARCANE [18]; FAST [19]; EWC-UL [12]; PNN-UL [20];
ForgetFS [21] (saliency-masked ascent); MULL [22] (SVD-projected updates). We also include
ForgetFS+TV and MULL+TV: the same baselines augmented with our spatial TV term, providing
a fair comparison of the TV contribution in isolation (Table 7). All baselines receive the same oracle
window [ts , te ] information as SSU-LSF.

Datasets, metrics, and confounding. All three benchmarks use author-injected confounding
(a limitation we acknowledge explicitly; Appendix O details deployment caveats and the window-
estimation sensitivity analysis). A real documented event is in Appendix N. The MIA uses LiRA-style
likelihood ratio tests (Appendix H), not logistic regression on NLL. CRR spans 0.77 (CropHarvest)
to 0.86 (ERA5) and fully automatic results in Appendix O.
Each benchmark uses a distinct metric triplet (definitions in Appendix G): NDVI-LST Fusion (3,847
locations, 2002–2022 [16]; Tc =24 mo): RMSE↓, CRR↑, TCI↑. ERA5 Patchified (1,920 patches,
1979–2022 [3]; Tc =24 mo): SKT-R↓ (K), MIA↓ (LiRA, chance= 0.50), ACC↑. CropHarvest
(87,343 locations [17]; Tc =6 mo): Sp-R↓, SCS↓ (FSS correlation r= − 0.91; Appendix I), LC-F1↑.

Ablation. Full 2×2 interaction ablations and sample-efficiency analysis are in Table 11 (Ap-
pendix Q). Key results: removing both Φ and KL simultaneously (i.e., plain gradient reversal with

EKFac (0.821), confirming that any Hessian approximation helps over pure gradient cosine similarity.

4   Conclusion
We develop SSU-LSF, the first machine-unlearning framework for Mamba-family SSMs in geosci-
entific land surface forecasting. Proposition 1’s monotone Tc prediction is empirically validated
(R2 =0.995 across Tc ∈ {6, 12, 24, 36} months), even though the bound itself is loose by ≈200×
due to Pinsker and Lipschitz looseness, a limitation we quantify explicitly. We find spatial TV
regularization (framed as parameter-space fused lasso) is the decisive factor for spatial coherence,
and EKFac-informed footprint localization is decisive for CRR.
Limitations. All primary experiments use injected confounding with oracle window knowledge. CRR
requires knowing affected locations (not deployable as stated). The bound is qualitatively predictive
but quantitatively vacuous at ρmax =0.91. Generalization beyond Mamba to S4 is demonstrated on
one benchmark only (Appendix P). Future work: automated change-point detection, real multi-event
archives, and tighter spectral bounds for selective SSMs.
Broader impact. SSU-LSF lets forecasting agencies correct biased models without full retraining. It
could be misused to erase inconvenient true signals; requiring explicit timestamps [ts , te ] and an MIA
audit mitigates this.

A      Extended Related Work
A.1     SSM-based geoscientific forecasting
VMamba [25] and SiMBA [26] extend Mamba to 2D vision and multivariate time series. We note that
architectures with higher ρ(Ā) are systematically more susceptible to confounding: the first term of Proposition 1,
O((1−ρTc )/((1−ρ)µ)), accumulates faster. PredRNN [27], EarthFormer [28], Pangu-Weather [29], and
GraphCast [30] provide ConvLSTM and Transformer baselines that Mamba surpasses computationally. None
addresses post-hoc bias removal.

A.2     Machine unlearning and spatial TV prior art
Machine unlearning was formalized by Cao and Yang [31]. Certified removal [32] is limited to linear models.
Rewind-to-Delete (R2D) [23] extends certified (ϵ, δ) unlearning to nonconvex models but needs a pre-deletion
checkpoint and Gaussian weight noise, and does not localize in time; rewinding 10% of training on ERA5
would cost ≈4.7 GPU-h before the noise-induced utility loss. SISA [13] requires pre-training partitioning.
ForgetFS [21] applies gradient-saliency masking; MULL [22] uses SVD on forget-gradient directions. Both lack
temporal indexing via spectral footprints. Parameter-space spatial TV used here is the fused-lasso formulation of
Tibshirani et al. [35] extended to patch adjacency graphs; it was introduced for 1D chains and applied to 2D grids
in compressed sensing. We use it for parameter smoothing across spatial patch embeddings, a straightforward
application, not a primary scientific contribution.

A.3     Non-stationarity
Change-point detection [33, 24] and concept drift [34] identify breaks but provide no parameter correction.
Domain adaptation requires a clean target dataset, unavailable retroactively. SSU-LSF requires only [ts , te ] and
the original training set.

H     MIA: LiRA Protocol
We use LiRA [37] (Likelihood Ratio Attack) with 64 shadow models per benchmark. Shadow models are trained
on random subsets of Dclean (temporal split disjoint from [ts , te ] by at least 12 months on all benchmarks).
LiRA computes a per-sample likelihood ratio Λi using the distribution of per-sample loss across shadow models;
membership is inferred by thresholding Λi . This is strictly more powerful than logistic regression on NLL
(the weak MIA used in prior versions). At LiRA, CM achieves MIA = 0.84 and SSU-LSF achieves 0.53.
As a retrain-referenced check, the output-space distance to the oracle, KL[p(·|θ̃)∥p(·|θ OR )] on the affected
ERA5 test window, is 0.212 (CM), 0.074 (GR), 0.043 (MULL), and 0.031 (SSU-LSF), agreeing with the LiRA
ranking.

M     Spectral Radius Distribution
Mean ρ(Ā) increases monotonically from block 1 (0.79) to block 6 (0.88) across 10 seeds (Table 10), consistent
with deeper blocks learning longer-range temporal dependencies. The maximum observed value ρmax =0.91
yields an unlearning cost bound of Lenc δKL /(1−ρmax ) ≈ 10.2 K (Proposition 1), vacuous relative to the oracle
ERA5 RMSE of 1.19 K; this is precisely the quantitative looseness acknowledged in Section 4. The sensitivity
analysis in Figure 2(b) corroborates this: at ρmax =0.91, δKL ≤ 0.07 is required to hold ∆RMSE%<5%,
whereas the mean ρ=0.84 permits δKL ≤ 0.10. As ρ → 1 both terms of Proposition 1 grow as 1/(1−ρ) and the
footprint widens (≈9.5 extra steps per side at ρ=0.95). Two mitigations work in practice: clipping ρ(Ā) ≤ 0.95
during ascent by rescaling ∆ (NDVI-LST: CRR 0.818, ∆RMSE 3.4%), and tightening δKL to ≤0.07 for blocks
with ρ>0.9.

O     Window Estimation Sensitivity and Deployment Limitations
Limitations of the evaluation setup. CRR as defined requires knowing the set of affected locations,
which is unavailable in retrospective deployment. A deployable variant would compute CRR over all locations,
diluting the metric for methods with geographic specificity. We recommend the spatial CRR heatmap over
change-point-detected patches as a more deployable diagnostic. The MIA (LiRA) requires shadow models
trained on the same distribution, which may be expensive to construct in production.

Window estimation sensitivity. Table 8 (last row) shows CRR degradation under ±3-month window error.
We recommend using PELT [24] with a minimum segment length of 6 months, which achieves median window
endpoint error of 1.8 months on synthetic benchmarks.

Fully automatic pipeline. With both the window and the affected locations estimated by PELT (NDVI-
LST, Tc =24), SSU-LSF gives CRR 0.789±0.016 and ∆RMSE 3.9%, against 0.821 and 3.5% with oracle
knowledge; window-only estimation gives 0.803 (Table 8). The location false-positive rate is 6.8%, which
mainly costs clean-domain RMSE.

We apply SSU-LSF to an S4 backbone (3 layers, d=256, ZOH discretization) trained on the NDVI-LST

Key interaction findings: (1) Removing both Φ and KL together (plain GR with TV) gives CRR 0.768, better
than removing Φ alone (0.694) but worse than the full model (0.821), confirming Φ and KL are complementary:
KL constrains the damage done by unspecific ascent, while Φ makes the ascent specific enough that KL barely
needs to fire. (2) SSU-LSF is sample-efficient: CRR saturates at |Dclean |=500, confirming the method is
lightweight in clean-data requirements.


R     Footprint Extension Derivation
The half-width of the footprint extension beyond [ts , te ] is derived
                                                                  P as follows. The influence of a confounding
step at t0 ∈ [ts , te ] on parameters via the recurrence decays as t>t0 ρt−t0 ≈ ρ/(1 − ρ) in expectation over t.
For a symmetric window, the mass in each tail (steps outside [ts , te ] that exceed threshold γ) is approximately
half this value, giving extension ≈ 0.5ρ/(1−ρ) steps on each side. At ρ=0.84: 0.5×0.84/0.16=2.6 additional
steps. For a Tc =24 month window, this is 2.6/24=11% extension, small but non-trivial.

S    Hyperparameter Sensitivity
CRR is robust to γ between the 85th and 97th percentile (Figure 2a); under-selection below the 80th misses
confounding steps and collapses CRR, while over-selection above the 98th draws clean steps into the ascent
and raises ∆RMSE% sharply. The KL tolerance δKL ∈ [0.02, 0.10] yields the best CRR–degradation trade-off
(Figure 2b); values above 0.15 permit catastrophic forgetting, visible as a ∆RMSE% spike exceeding 18%.
The retention weight β ∈ [0.5, 0.7] is optimal across all three benchmarks (Figure 2c), differing by at most 0.05
between benchmarks, confirming the defaults transfer with minimal re-tuning. For the TV weight (Table 12),
SCS falls steeply up to λTV =0.01 and then flattens, while λTV ≥0.03 starts to cost CRR and clean-domain
RMSE; 0.01 is the knee.
